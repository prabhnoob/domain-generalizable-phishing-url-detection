    This course is project driven. Students will work in teams to define, develop, and complete a project in the field
    of data mining/machine learning over the course of one semester. Each team will consist of undergraduate
    students and one graduate student, who serves as the project lead, coordinating the project and guide the team
    through the research and development process. The course project has two main objectives:

         -   Hands-on experience: Gain practical experience in applying data mining and machine learning methods
             to a real-world problem, while developing skills in project planning, implementation, evaluation, and
             teamwork.
         -   Research experience: Develop an understanding of the research process, from identifying a problem
             and reviewing existing approaches to designing, implementing, and evaluating a solution.

    Projects should aim to advance the state of the art, rather than simply reproduce an existing solution. This
    provides students with an opportunity to conduct meaningful research while completing the course requirements.

    A research project typically starts with a problem and proposes a solution by designing, implementing, and
    evaluating an algorithm or computational approach. In this sense, it may resemble a software project; however,
    there are important differences. A software project may primarily focus on implementing or integrating existing
    algorithms to build a functional system. In contrast, a research project should aim to investigate a problem,
    identify a limitation or gap in existing approaches, and propose a solution that addresses that gap. The proposed
    solution may involve (1) a new algorithm, (2) a modification or extension of an existing algorithm, (3) a new
    combination/pipeline of existing techniques, or (4) a novel application of existing methods to a problem where
    they have not been adequately explored. Therefore, the goal is not simply to implement an existing method, but
    to make a meaningful contribution beyond what has already been done, supported by appropriate experiments
    and evaluation. Roughly, a research project consists of the following milestones (phases):

    1. Project Definition & Team Formation (Proposal)
    2. Background & Related Work (Literature Review)
    3. Solution Design & Implementation (Proposed Solution or Method)
    4. Evaluation (Experimentation)
    5. Project Presentation: 3-Minute Talk (3MT)
    6. Project Management & Documentation (Graduate Student Only, Umbrella Activity)

    A project manual has been prepared to guide students through each milestone. Each milestone breaks the project
    into a series of smaller steps and deliverables. The current manual covers Milestone 4: Evaluation, also known
    as Experiments or Experimentation.

    evaluation is empirical assessment. Rather than mathematically proving that an algorithm is correct or
    theoretically demonstrating that its time complexity is optimal, experiments are conducted using datasets, data
    mining (machine learning) models, evaluation metrics, and comparative analyses. Evaluation strategy
    investigates:

       -    Does the proposed approach achieve satisfactory classification performance?
       -    Does it outperform conventional classification models?
       -    How sensitive is its performance to different hyperparameter settings?
       -    Does it perform consistently across different datasets or experimental configurations?

    These questions translate into experiments that allow assessing the proposed approach against measurable
    criteria. The central idea is simple: the claims must be supported by experimental evidence. For this course, your
    evaluation should primarily consist of four components:

       1.   Research Questions (RQs): What exactly do you want to investigate?
       2.   Datasets: What data will you use to conduct your experiments?
       3.   Baselines: Which existing methods will you compare against?
       4.   Results: How will you conduct experiments, measure performance, and interpret the findings?

    The following sections explain each component and how to incorporate it into your project report.

                                            4.2. Research Questions (RQs)

    Research Questions (RQs) are specific questions that define what you want to investigate through your
    experiments. A research question should be sufficiently specific that you can design an experiment to answer it.
    Ideally, each RQ should correspond to one or more experimental analyses and lead to measurable findings.
    Common RQs are:

       1. RQ1 (Inference Performance): How does the proposed approach perform compared with conventional
          approaches?

    Here, the proposed approach refers to your project's main contribution, which may take several forms, including:

       -    A new model or algorithm: Proposing a new learning or prediction method.
       -    A new embedding or representation method: Introducing a different way of representing data instances,
            such as words, documents, users, or graphs, before applying an existing machine learning model.
       -    Feature engineering or feature augmentation: Introducing new features, combining existing features,
            or investigating the impact of additional information on predictive performance.
       -    Method adaptation: Modifying or extending an existing method to improve its performance or
            suitability for a particular problem.
       -    A new application domain: Applying existing machine learning methods to a new domain, problem, or
            dataset, and investigating their effectiveness in that context.

    Therefore, your evaluation should be designed around the actual contribution of your project. For example,

       -    If a new embedding method, you should investigate whether the resulting representations improve
            downstream task (e.g., classification performance.)
       -    If a set of additional features, you should compare performance with and without those features.
       -    If applying existing methods to a new domain, you should investigate how effectively these methods
            perform in that new domain.

       2. RQ2 (Hyperparameter Study): How do different hyperparameter configurations affect the performance
          of the proposed approach?

       3. RQ3 (Efficiency (Optional)): How does the computational running time of the proposed approach
          compare with the competing methods?

    Create a subsection entitled Research Questions in your report. For each RQ:

       -   State the research question explicitly.
       -   Briefly explain why the question is relevant to your project.
       -   Identify the experiment(s) that will help answer it.

    For example:
                      “RQ1: Can non-parametric classifiers outperform parameteric ones in the
                      task of author’s gender identification for a social post? Answering this
                      question provides insight about the non-parametric model’s capability to
                      capture potentially nonlinear patterns in social media writing styles
                      compared with parametric classifiers when identifying an author's gender.
                      We address this question by comparing k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) as the
                      non-parametric and Logistic Regression as the parametric baselines.

                      RQ2: Is the efficacy of non-parametric classifiers consistent across social
                      posts from different platforms? Our objective is to investigate whether the
                      predictive performance of non-parametric classifiers generalizes across
                      platforms with potentially different writing styles, user behaviours, and
                      content characteristics. We perform a comparative analysis using datasets
                      from two platforms, namely, Facebook and LinkedIn.

                      RQ3: How do different hyperparameter configurations affect the
                      predictive performance of non-parametric classifiers? Our objective is to
                      investigate the consistency of non-parametric classifiers across their
                      hyperparameter settings and identify configurations that yield the best
                      predictive performance. We conduct experiments using different values of
                      k in k-NN and evaluate their performance through n-fold cross-
                      validation.”

    Important: Your RQs should guide the organization of your experiments and results. Avoid introducing
    experimental results that do not help answer your stated research questions.

                                                 4.3. Benchmark Datasets

    A dataset is a collection of instances used to train, validate, and/or evaluate a data mining (machine learning)
    model. For example, in “the author gender classification” task, a dataset may contain thousands of “social
    posts”, each associated with a “gender label” such as “female”, “male”, or “unknown”. The quality and
    characteristics of a dataset directly influence the reliability of the experimental findings. A model that performs
    well on one dataset may not necessarily perform equally well on another. Therefore, selecting and describing
    your datasets is an essential part of the evaluation strategy.
4




    4.3.1. Dataset Selection. You may use publicly available datasets, established benchmark datasets, or datasets
    collected and prepared as part of your project. Whenever possible, prefer publicly available datasets that have
    already been used in related research. Such datasets make it easier to compare your findings with existing work.
    You should also consider:

          •    Relevance: Does the dataset represent the problem you are investigating?
          •    Size: Does it contain a sufficient number of instances for meaningful experimentation?
          •    Representativeness: Does it cover the different types of instances you expect to encounter?
          •    Label availability: For supervised learning, does it contain the ground-truth labels required for training
               and evaluation?
          •    Accessibility: Can other researchers access the dataset and reproduce your experiments?

    There is no requirement to use a state-of-the-art benchmark dataset. However, you must justify why your selected
    datasets are appropriate for your research problem.

    4.3.1. Dataset Statistics. You should provide a concise statistical description of every dataset used in your
    experiments. Depending on your problem, useful statistics may include:

          -    Number of instances (dataset size)
          -    Number of features
          -    Class labels
          -    Class distribution: number or percentage of instances per class
          -    Missing values: number or percentage of incomplete records
          -    Dataset source: link, license, repository, or collection procedure

                                                       4.4. Baselining

    A baseline is an existing method used as a reference point to assess the performance of a proposed approach. A
    baseline helps answer a fundamental question: Is the proposed approach actually better than existing alternatives?
    Without comparative evaluation, it is difficult to establish the value of the proposed approach. For example, in
    a classification task, data mining (machine learning) classifiers, like those covered in this course including k-
    Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) and Logistic Regression (LR), allows investigating how the proposed approach
    performs against two established and conceptually different classification approaches. You are encouraged to
    include as much as possible baselines including methods like tree-based classifiers (Decision Tree, Random
    Forests), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Naive Bayes.

    Optionally, you may also investigate methods reported in related research papers, particularly strong or recent
    approaches. However, including state-of-the-art methods is not a requirement for this course. The primary
    objective is to understand and practice the experimental methodology of comparative evaluation. Create a
    subsection entitled Baselines and for each baseline:

          -    Identify the model and briefly explain how it works.
          -    Explain why it is a suitable competitor for your proposed approach.
          -    Identify its important hyperparameters.
          -    Explain the implementation or library used (e.g., Scikit-learn).
          -    Provide references when the model or implementation is taken from existing work.

    4.4.1. Fair Comparison. A meaningful comparison requires that all methods are evaluated under comparable
    experimental conditions: that is, same datasets, preprocessing, training-validation-test partitions, and evaluation
    metrics. Avoid giving one method access to information unavailable to the other methods. Tune each model
    using only the training and validation data, not the final test set. Remember that different models may require
    different preprocessing. For example, feature scaling is important for k-NN and often useful for LR. Such
    differences are acceptable as long as the preprocessing is appropriate for each model and does not introduce an
    unfair advantage. Here is an example4:

                                          4.5. Evaluation Strategy and Metrics

    This is the most important part of your evaluation. Here, you demonstrate how you conducted your experiments.
    Your results should not simply be a collection of accuracy values. They should provide experimental evidence
    that answers your Research Questions. Explain the experimental procedure in the following steps:

    4.5.1. Dataset Splits: Before conducting experiments, divide your dataset into two independent and randomly
    selected portions:

       -   Development Set: Used for model training, cross-validation, and hyperparameter selection.
       -   Final Test Set: Used only once the model configurations have been finalized, to evaluate their
           generalization performance on unseen data.

    A typical division is: 80% development set and 20% final test set. You may use another reasonable ratio
    depending on the dataset size, but you must justify your choice. Use stratified splitting whenever appropriate to
    preserve the class distribution across the partitions.

    Important: Never use the final test set to select hyperparameters, choose models, or make other experimental
    decisions. Doing so introduces data leakage and makes the final performance estimates unreliable.

    4.5.2. Metric: Evaluation metrics quantify the performance of a machine learning model. The appropriate
    metrics depend on the task. For classification task, metrics include the following in the order of importance:

       •   Confusion Matrix: A breakdown of correct and incorrect predictions across classes.                           Commented [A1]: Highly recommended but takes a
       •   AUC: A measure of the model's ability to discriminate between classes across classification thresholds.      large space.
       •   F1-Score: The harmonic mean of precision and recall.                                                         Commented [A2]: The best!
       •   Precision: The proportion of predicted positive instances that are actually positive.
7




          •   Recall: The proportion of actual positive instances that are correctly identified.
          •   Accuracy: The proportion of correctly classified instances.                                              Commented [A3]: Not recommended.

    You do not necessarily need to report every metric. Select metrics that are relevant to your research questions
    and explain why they are appropriate. For imbalanced classification datasets, accuracy alone is misleading. In
    such cases, precision, recall, F1-score, and the confusion matrix can provide a more informative assessment. For
    multiclass classification, clearly state whether you report macro, micro, or weighted averages when using
    precision, recall, and F1-score.

    4.5.3. Conduct n-Fold Cross-Validation: Once you have separated the final test set, use n-fold cross-
    validation on the development set to investigate model configurations. In n-fold cross-validation, the
    development set is divided into n approximately equal-sized folds. For example, in 5-fold cross-validation:

          -   Divide the development set into five folds.
          -   Train the model on four folds and validate it on the remaining fold.
          -   Repeat the experiment five times, using a different fold for validation each time.
          -   Calculate the average performance across the five experiments.
          -   Each instance is therefore used for validation once and for training four times.
          -   The average performance provides a more reliable estimate than relying on a single arbitrary
              training/validation split.

    You must use the same cross-validation partitioning across all models. Sample python scripts to implement n-
    fold cross-valiation have been attached to this manual, als can be find here5,6.

    4.5.4. Conduct Hyperparameter Studies: A hyperparameter is a configuration value that controls the
    behavior of a machine learning model but is not directly learned from the training data. For example, in k-NN,
    the number of neighbors (k), in LR, training hyperparameters like learning rate, number of epoch, batch size,
    inference threshold, and in a Decision Tree, the maximum tree depth are hyperparameters. Different
    hyperparameter configurations may lead to substantially different results. Therefore, you should investigate how
    the performance of methods changes under different configurations. You should choose reasonable ranges based
    on the model, dataset, and problem. For every configuration, evaluate the model using the same n-fold cross-
    validation procedure. You then report the average performance across the folds.

    The best hyperparameter configuration is the configuration that achieves the best performance according to the
    selected metric. For example, if your primary metric is F1, select the configuration with the highest average
    cross-validation F1.

    Important: You do not need to investigate every possible combination of hyperparameters. A reasonable and
    well-justified search is sufficient for this course. For example, consider the following experiments:

                                            4.6. Results, Attribution, and Discussion

    Present your findings using tables, charts, and other appropriate visualizations. Your results section should be
    organized around your Research Questions.

    For RQ1 (Comparative Performance), present a table comparing models’ performance on the final test set using
    their best hyperparameters. Explain

          -    Which method achieves the best performance?
          -    How much improvement does your proposed method achieve?
          -    Are there particular metrics where another method performs better?
          -    [Attribution: Why] What might explain the observed differences?


    For RQ2 (Hyperparameter Sensitivity), present a chart or table showing how different hyperparameter
    configurations affect your proposed method. Explain:

          -    Which configuration performs best?
          -    How sensitive is the model to the selected parameter?
          -    Does increasing or decreasing the parameter improve performance?
          -    Is the model relatively stable across different configurations?
          -    [Attribution: Why] What might explain the observed differences?

    Important: Do not simply present tables and expect readers to interpret them. Every important result should be
    accompanied by an explanation of what it means and how it answers the corresponding research question. Here
    is an example7:
                  “In response to RQ1, i.e., whether seq-to-seq models yield better
                  performance vs. feedforward models, the results from Table 3 demonstrate
                  that all seq-to-seq models outperform the feedforward ones across metrics
                  and datasets. This can be attributed to conditioning of expert
                  recommendation on the previously recommended experts in the output
